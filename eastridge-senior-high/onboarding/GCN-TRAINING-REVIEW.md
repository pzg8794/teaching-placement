## AED

### What to remember

- Know where the building's AED is located before an emergency occurs.
- Sudden cardiac arrest means the heart unexpectedly stops pumping blood effectively. A person may suddenly collapse, be unresponsive, breathe abnormally, and have no pulse.
- Warning signs can include a racing or irregular heartbeat, dizziness, unusual fainting or seizures, chest discomfort during exercise, excessive shortness of breath, and extreme or unexpected fatigue.
- Cardiac arrest is immediately life-threatening: brain injury can begin within minutes, and survival decreases roughly 10 percent for every untreated minute.
- An AED analyzes heart rhythm and gives voice or visual prompts. It delivers a shock only when the detected rhythm calls for one.
- Early CPR and AED use are part of the chain of survival. An AED used within the first three to five minutes can greatly improve survival.
- For adult CPR, use hard, fast chest compressions at 100–120 per minute and a depth of at least two inches. Switch compressors when possible to maintain quality.
- If someone collapses, activate emergency response immediately: call 911, give the exact location and best entrance, send someone to retrieve the AED, and send someone to direct responders from the entrance.
- Keep CPR going while the AED is retrieved and prepared. After a shock, resume CPR immediately for about two minutes (five cycles) before the next analysis.
- Follow the device prompts, attach the pads to the bare chest as illustrated, and ensure everyone is clear of the person during rhythm analysis and before a shock.
- Move the person away from standing water and dry the chest before attaching pads. Keep pads at least one inch from implanted devices or piercings.
- Do not use an AED on someone who has a pulse. Follow emergency-dispatcher guidance when trauma is involved.
- The five-step chain of survival is: recognize and activate emergency response; begin high-quality CPR; use the AED rapidly; obtain advanced resuscitation/EMS support; and provide post-resuscitation care.
- In a school, AEDs should be visible, unlocked, near a phone, centrally placed, maintained and tested, and reachable within approximately three minutes round-trip.
- CPR/AED training is strongly recommended even though modern AED instructions are designed so a responder can follow them under stress.

### School-use cue

**Recognize, call, compress, locate, attach, clear, follow prompts.**

## Dignity for All Students Act / Code of Conduct NY overview

### What to remember

- DASA is primarily preventive: create a safe, respectful climate through early intervention, education, and clear expectations—not punishment alone.
- The protections apply to harassment, bullying, or discrimination by students or employees on school property or at school functions, and to off-campus conduct that foreseeably risks substantial disruption in school.
- Protected characteristics include actual or perceived race, color, weight, national origin, ethnic group, religion or religious practice, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, and sex.
- Use the terms and pronouns students use for themselves. Language can vary across culture, age, region, and individual identity.
- The Code of Conduct establishes expectations grounded in civility, mutual respect, citizenship, character, tolerance, honesty, and integrity. It includes cyberbullying and relevant off-campus conduct.
- Each building has a trained DASA coordinator. Know who that person is at Eastridge and follow the district's referral process.
- If you witness or receive a report of harassment, bullying, or discrimination, notify the principal, superintendent, or designee orally within **one school day**.
- Submit the corresponding written report within **two school days after the oral report**.
- The CROWN Act extends race protections to traits historically associated with race, including hair texture and protective hairstyles.
- Staff share a legal and moral responsibility to maintain safety, respect, and support for every student.

### School-use cue

**Interrupt safely, support the student, preserve facts, report orally within one school day, and document within two school days.**

## Mental Illness and Disorders Awareness for Educators

### What to remember

- Federal data cited in the module estimate that **13–20%** of U.S. children experience a mental disorder in a given year.
- Look for meaningful changes across three areas: physical or routine changes, persistent emotional changes, and behavioral or academic changes.
- Concern rises when behavior is developmentally unusual, persistent or severe, and disrupts school, relationships, or daily functioning.
- Ask how the student is doing and listen compassionately. Educators observe, document, support, and refer; they do not diagnose or provide treatment.
- Treat statements involving suicide, self-harm, hopelessness, or danger as urgent and follow the school's immediate escalation procedures.
- Early identification, intervention, and appropriate support can help adolescents lead productive and fulfilling lives.

### School-use cue

**Notice patterns, ask, listen, document facts, refer, and escalate immediate danger.**

## Toileting and Diapering — NY

### What to remember

- New York's statewide school protocols require timely restroom access, safe and accessible facilities, privacy, hygiene, supervision, supplies, and annual staff training.
- A student cannot be denied enrollment, suspended, excluded, punished, or shamed because of toileting status, accidents, disposable training pants, or a need for assistance.
- Assistance must be respectful, culturally responsive, developmentally appropriate, disability-inclusive, and consistent with the student's IEP, Section 504 Plan, or Individualized Healthcare Plan.
- Build independence when possible. Explain what you are doing, seek verbal or nonverbal consent before helping, respect modesty and boundaries, and never discuss the student's needs around peers or unrelated people.
- Two trained adults are ideal during intimate assistance. When that is not feasible, use approved safeguards that protect both privacy and safety. Other students must never participate.
- Use a dedicated, clean changing area away from food preparation. Never leave a student unattended on an elevated surface, and use only transfer methods and equipment for which staff have been properly trained.
- Follow universal precautions: use appropriate PPE, wash hands after possible body-fluid exposure, sanitize the surface before and after use, contain soiled items, and coordinate safe disposal and supply replenishment with custodial staff.
- During diapering or toileting, move from front to back, dispose of wipes and gloves correctly, restore clothing, clean the area, and document the procedure and the student's tolerance when required.
- Notify the appropriate licensed healthcare professional about skin breakdown, blood, strong odor, unusual discharge, marks, or other concerning signs. Follow mandated-reporter duties if abuse or neglect is suspected.
- Incontinence can have medical, developmental, neurological, medication-related, stress-related, constipation-related, or trauma-related causes. Do not assume misconduct or blame the student.

**Protect dignity, explain and obtain assent, use PPE, clean safely, document, and report concerns.**

## Bloodborne Pathogens

### What to remember

- The main workplace bloodborne pathogens are hepatitis B (HBV), hepatitis C (HCV), and HIV. Transmission requires infectious blood or other potentially infectious material to enter through broken skin, mucous membranes, or a puncture wound.
- HIV is not spread through ordinary contact, shared food, toilets, tools, perspiration, saliva, or insects. Dried blood can still present an HBV risk, so use precautions regardless of appearance.
- **Universal precautions:** treat all blood and hard-to-identify body fluids as potentially infectious.
- Common school exposure situations include first aid, bloody noses and cuts, toileting or menstrual assistance, biting or scratching, sports and laboratory injuries, spill cleanup, and custodial or bio-waste work.
- Use engineering controls, safe work practices, and PPE. Wash hands after removing PPE; clean and disinfect contaminated surfaces; never pick up broken glass or sharps by hand.
- Place sharps in a puncture-resistant, leak-proof, biohazard-marked sharps container. Mark biological hazards with the orange-red biohazard symbol.
- For spills, remove bulk material and use an appropriate EPA-registered disinfectant at its labeled concentration and contact time. The module gives a 1:10 bleach solution as an alternative when commercial products are unavailable.
- Wear gloves whenever hand contact with blood or infectious material is possible. Add eye, face, and clothing protection when splashing or spraying is possible. Inspect gloves first; a face shield does not replace goggles or safety glasses.
- Remove gloves without touching the contaminated exterior to bare skin. Never wash disposable gloves for reuse; decontaminate reusable utility gloves only when intact.
- If exposure occurs, report it immediately and contact the supervisor or custodial personnel. Avoid direct contact, contain further contamination, and follow the employer's Exposure Control Plan.
- The employer must provide confidential post-exposure medical evaluation and follow-up at no cost, document the exposure route and source when feasible, offer testing and counseling, and provide the healthcare professional's written opinion within 15 days after evaluation completion.
- The Exposure Control Plan covers exposure determination, methods of compliance, vaccination, post-exposure response, hazard communication, training, and recordkeeping.

### School-use cue

**Assume infectious, protect, contain, clean, report immediately, and obtain confidential follow-up.**

## Peer Counseling, Anti-Violence, and Conflict Resolution Programs

### What to remember

- School threats can include physical aggression, bullying, hazing, harassment, gangs, cyberbullying, and weapon-related violence. A proactive, caring school climate is more effective than relying only on reaction and punishment.
- Do not rely on a single perpetrator “profile.” Warning behavior and context matter more. Serious targeted violence is often planned and communicated over time rather than occurring as an unexplained snap decision.
- Peer counseling trains a diverse group of students to listen, help, mentor, tutor, welcome newcomers, mediate lower-level conflicts, and refer concerns. A trained counselor, social worker, or other qualified adult must supervise the program.
- Peer helpers need explicit instruction in communication, problem-solving, decision-making, prevention, confidentiality boundaries, and—critically—when to involve trained professionals.
- Anti-violence strategies include school management and discipline, environmental safeguards, and educational or curriculum-based prevention. No single program fits every school.
- Choose interventions using the school's actual needs, demographics, resources, and evidence. Include students, staff, families, community agencies, and emergency services; monitor results and adjust over time.
- Conflict is a normal part of life, and peaceful conflict resolution is learned. Strong programs train and support the whole school community, not just a small student group.
- Six mediation steps: agree to mediate; tell each story; identify interests and needs; generate win-win possibilities; evaluate options; create an agreement.
- Five core skills: active listening, clear communication, avoiding blame, staying calm, and collaborating.
- Effective prevention is a long-term commitment: shared behavioral standards, early academic and behavioral support, consistent responses, progressively focused intervention, and collaboration with outside professionals and agencies.

### School-use cue

**Listen early, distinguish peer help from professional intervention, address needs without blame, and escalate threats immediately.**

## School Safety — NY

### What to remember

- School emergency planning covers severe weather, fire, suspicious items or bomb threats, utility or structural failures, transportation accidents, intruders, weapons, hostages, hazardous materials, public-health emergencies, and other crises.
- Under New York's SAVE law, every public-school district must maintain both a district-wide safety plan and a building-level emergency response plan.
- The district-wide plan is public and includes the chain of command, communication with authorities and families, procedures for people with special needs, and annual training.
- The building-level plan is facility-specific and **confidential**. It includes the response team, evacuation procedures, first-responder access to maps and floor plans, drills, and coordination with local emergency agencies.
- Know and follow the district Code of Conduct. It addresses expectations, reporting, discipline, family notification, disability-law compliance, and when law enforcement is notified.
- A disruptive student substantially interferes with instruction or teacher authority; a violent student commits or threatens violence, possesses or threatens a weapon, or intentionally destroys property.
- Classroom removal must follow district procedures. State the reason, allow the student's explanation, report the removal to the principal, and follow the required family-notification and conference process.
- During lockdown, follow the building's exact protocol: secure the room, reduce visibility, stop activity, account for present/missing/extra students, and await official directions. People outside evacuate to the designated off-campus location.
- Firearms on school grounds are prohibited unless narrowly authorized in writing under applicable law. District security personnel must meet required training and legal standards.
- Safety plans and Codes of Conduct are reviewed annually. Schools also report violent incidents and discipline data to the state.
- Emergency-response training includes violence prevention and mental health. New employees must receive it within 30 days of hire; annual staff certification is due by September 15 or within 30 days of hire, whichever is sooner.

### School-use cue

**Know the building plan, follow the chain of command, secure and account, communicate only through official channels, and document required actions.**

## Sexual Harassment NY — State Mandated

### What to remember so far

- Every New York employee is entitled to a workplace free from sexual harassment. The employer's policy must be provided in the employee's spoken language and reviewed periodically.
- The policy must prohibit harassment, give examples, explain state/federal rights and remedies, provide a complaint form, establish a timely and confidential investigation process with due process, identify available forums, state sanctions, and prohibit retaliation.
- Protections cover employees, supervisors, subordinates, applicants, paid and unpaid interns, contractors, vendors, customers, visitors, and others conducting business, regardless of immigration status.
- Sexual harassment includes conduct based on sex, sexual orientation, perceived or actual sex, gender expression, gender identity, or transgender status. It can occur in person, during work travel or events, or through messages, email, social media, and remote-work platforms.
- New York does **not** require conduct to be severe or pervasive. Intent is not controlling; the question is how a reasonable person with the same protected characteristics would experience the conduct.
- A hostile environment can involve sexual or sex-based words, jokes, signs, pranks, intimidation, unwanted contact, or violence that creates inferior working conditions.
- Quid pro quo occurs when a supervisor or manager links hiring, promotion, continued employment, or another job benefit to sexual cooperation.
- Prohibited conduct can include assault, unwanted touching, sexual propositions or pressure, threats tied to work benefits, sexual jokes or remarks, demeaning displays, pornography, and sex stereotyping.

### School-use cue

**Notice impact, not claimed intent; document, report through policy channels, protect confidentiality, and never retaliate.**

## Student Data Privacy — NY Education Law §2-d

### What to remember

- FERPA and New York Education Law §2-d protect personally identifiable student information. Collect and transmit the least information needed for a legitimate educational purpose.
- Parents generally may inspect education records within 45 days, request correction of inaccurate or misleading records, consent before disclosure, opt out of directory-information designation, and file complaints.
- FERPA permits limited disclosure without consent—for example, to school officials with legitimate educational interests, a receiving school, certain contracted service providers, authorized studies, or designated government authorities.
- Directory information can include names, contact information, photographs, grade level, attendance, activities, honors, and similar data, but families may refuse directory designation.
- PII includes direct identifiers and combinations of indirect details that would let a reasonable person identify the student. Properly de-identified, aggregated, or anonymized data that cannot identify a student is not PII.
- PPRA governs certain federally funded student surveys and evaluations; COPPA governs online services collecting personal information from children under 13.
- Third-party contracts must specify authorized purposes, access and subcontractor limits, storage and security protections, encryption, contract duration, disposal or return of data, and procedures for challenging accuracy.
- Contractors may use data only for the contracted educational purpose, limit access to people with legitimate educational interests, maintain administrative/technical/physical safeguards, and may not sell or disclose PII for marketing.
- A breach must be reported to NYSED as quickly as possible and without unreasonable delay; parents must then be notified promptly. Violations can lead to fines, required training, or loss of data access for up to five years.
- Complete student records are maintained by the school or district, making the local school the usual first point for access or amendment requests.
- Student PII cannot be sold or released for commercial or marketing purposes. Parents may inspect or receive copies, request amendments, and complain locally, to NYSED's Chief Privacy Officer, or to the federal FERPA office.

## Child Abuse

### What to remember

- Five overlapping categories: physical abuse, sexual abuse, sexual exploitation, neglect, and emotional maltreatment. A child may experience more than one at once.
- Look for patterns and inconsistencies—not one isolated sign. Physical indicators can include unexplained injuries, patterned burns or bruises, injuries in unusual locations, or explanations that conflict with the evidence.
- Behavioral signs may include fear of caregivers or adults, avoiding home, unusual discomfort with touch, drastic behavior changes, withdrawal, aggression, sexualized behavior, self-harm, or a direct/indirect disclosure.
- Sexual abuse often has no obvious physical signs. A child's report is one of the strongest indicators: take it seriously and err on the side of protection.
- Sexual exploitation involves exchanging sexual activity involving a child for money, shelter, food, or another thing of value. Possible indicators include unexplained money, older controlling associates, injuries, branding tattoos, withdrawal, or abrupt changes.
- Neglect can involve inadequate food, clothing, shelter, healthcare, hygiene, education, or supervision. Consider the child's developmental level, the environment, and the persistence and impact of the condition.
- Emotional maltreatment involves repeated rejection, humiliation, domination, indifference, or criticism that harms development. Focus on persistent adverse effects on the child.
- Development matters: accidental bruises are common over bony areas in young children, but bruises behind the ears or on the torso are less typical. Very young children and children with disabilities can be especially vulnerable and may have difficulty communicating what happened.
- If a child discloses: control your shock; sit nearby; do not touch without permission; listen; affirm that the child is not to blame; ask only basic non-leading who/what/when/where questions needed for the report.
- Do not promise secrecy or outcomes. Explain what happens next, share only with people who can help, assess immediate safety, and document the child's words verbatim.
- Follow the district procedure and report suspected abuse immediately—this module states within 24 hours. Do not delay your mandated duty while trying to investigate or prove the allegation yourself.
- Good-faith reporters have confidentiality and legal protections; knowingly malicious false reporting is not protected.

### School-use cue

**Notice, listen without leading, protect immediate safety, document exact words, and report—do not investigate.**

## Workplace Violence in Schools — NY

### What to remember

- Workplace violence includes physical violence, threats, harassment, intimidation, stalking, and other aggressive behavior connected to work—not only physical fights.
- Four sources: a stranger committing a crime; a student/parent/visitor or service recipient; a current or former coworker; or someone with a personal relationship to an employee.
- A workplace includes any location outside the employee's home where work duties occur: school buildings, buses, field trips, sporting events, conferences, and off-site meetings.
- Warning signs can include major changes in behavior or mood, withdrawal, anger or resentment, recurring conflict, bullying, or escalating threats. Warning signs are prompts to report and assess—not diagnoses.
- Risk exists at several levels: community conditions; school access/security and anti-bullying systems; organizational culture, leadership, and communication; and individual mental-health, conflict, or staff-stress factors.
- Prevention combines counseling and support, crisis protocols, conflict-resolution programs, clear reporting systems, training, and a current emergency response plan.
- In East Irondequoit, report workplace-violence incidents promptly to a building administrator or immediate supervisor. If unsure, err on the side of reporting.
- For immediate life-threatening danger, take protective action and call 911 while someone alerts supervisors. For other incidents, let administrators determine whether law enforcement or internal personnel should respond.
- After an incident, complete the electronic Workplace Violence Report Form in the Human Resources area of EI Net. The district uses reports and stakeholder review to improve the prevention plan.
- For a serious unresolved program violation, first notify a supervisor in writing and allow reasonable time for correction. Imminent danger goes to local authorities immediately.
- If the serious violation remains unresolved, the employee or authorized representative may submit a signed, detailed written request for NYSDOL/PESH inspection; names may be withheld from the district copy.
- PESH information and complaints are available through the NY Department of Labor, including 1-844-SAFE-NYS.

### School-use cue

**Immediate danger: protect and call 911. Otherwise: report promptly, document, use the district form, and escalate unresolved safety violations.**
