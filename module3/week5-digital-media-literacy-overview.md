# Module 3 / Week 5 Reading Overview

## Digital & Media Literacy Through the Puzzle Plan Lens

**Course:** EDU498  
**Topic:** Week 5: Digital & Media Literacy  
**Purpose:** This overview connects the Week 5 readings and assigned media to the course Puzzle Plan theme: digital literacy as access, identity work, critical participation, and equity for students navigating neurodivergence, CLD identity, chronic illness, mental health needs, trauma, and intersecting conditions.

---

## Big-Picture Frame

Week 5 expands literacy beyond reading and writing into the digital and media practices students use to communicate, create, research, organize, critique, and participate in community. Digital literacy is not only a technical skill. It is also a social, cultural, political, and emotional practice.

The central question for this module is:

> How can digital and media literacy become a tool for access, agency, identity affirmation, and critical awareness rather than another system that rewards only speed, compliance, polished language, and invisible privilege?

For the Puzzle Plan, digital literacy matters because students do not all enter school with the same access to devices, language dominance, health stability, emotional safety, cultural recognition, or adult support. Digital tools can either reproduce those inequities or help interrupt them.

---

## Recommended Three Readings

The assignment asks students to choose 3 of the 6 readings. The three strongest readings for the Puzzle Plan are:

1. **Price-Dennis, Holmes, & Smith (2015)** — inclusive digital literacy practices.
2. **Stornaiuolo & Thomas (2017)** — youth digital activism and educational inequality.
3. **Melo-Pfeifer & Gertz (2022)** — disinformation about minorities and critical intercultural news literacy.

Together, these readings form a useful progression:

- **Access:** Who gets to participate in digital literacy?
- **Agency:** How do students use digital tools to speak back to systems?
- **Criticality:** How do students analyze media, stereotypes, misinformation, and harm?

---

## Reading 1: Price-Dennis, Holmes, & Smith (2015)

**Citation:** Price-Dennis, D., Holmes, K. A., & Smith, E. (2015). *Exploring digital literacy practices in an inclusive classroom*. *The Reading Teacher, 69*(2), 195-205.

This reading is central because it places digital literacy inside an inclusive classroom. Technology is not treated as a reward, decoration, or add-on. Instead, digital tools become part of how students access curriculum, represent meaning, and participate with others.

For the Puzzle Plan, this reading works as the **access layer**. If literacy is measured only through written output, silent compliance, fast processing, or linear organization, then many students appear less capable than they are. But when literacy includes multimodal creation, visual design, oral explanation, collaboration, and digital composition, students have more ways to show what they understand.

A classroom shaped by this reading would ask:

- Can students show understanding through writing, drawing, recording, coding, presenting, building, or explaining?
- Can digital tools reduce unnecessary barriers without reducing rigor?
- Are students using technology only to consume information, or are they creating and transforming meaning?
- Do students have meaningful roles that allow them to contribute in different ways?

This connects to teaching placement examples where students used circuits, Minecraft, and airplane/fan-circuit design to learn through building, explaining, helping, and trying again.

---

## Reading 2: Stornaiuolo & Thomas (2017)

**Citation:** Stornaiuolo, A., & Thomas, E. E. (2017). *Disrupting educational inequalities through youth digital activism*. *Review of Research in Education, 41*(1), 337-357.

This reading reframes youth digital activity as civic, cultural, and political literacy. Students are not simply consuming digital content; they are using digital spaces to organize, advocate, create counter-narratives, and participate in social change.

For the Puzzle Plan, this reading is important because it challenges deficit views of students who may be misunderstood by schools. A student who does not fit traditional measures of classroom success may still be developing complex literacies online: analyzing injustice, building community, translating identity, creating media, and speaking back to dominant narratives.

A classroom shaped by this reading would invite students to:

- analyze a social issue affecting their community,
- create a digital campaign or awareness artifact,
- compare how media sources represent marginalized groups,
- produce counter-narratives through video, audio, infographic, podcast, or multimodal writing,
- reflect on ethical participation and digital responsibility.

The key idea is that digital literacy is not only about using technology correctly. It is about using digital tools to claim identity, agency, and voice.

---

## Reading 3: Melo-Pfeifer & Gertz (2022)

**Citation:** Melo-Pfeifer, S., & Gertz, H. (2022). *Transforming disinformation on minorities into a pedagogical resource: Towards a critical intercultural news literacy*. *Media and Communication, 10*(4), 338-346.

This reading focuses on disinformation about minorities and shows how harmful media can become a resource for critical intercultural news literacy. It moves media literacy beyond basic fact-checking and asks students to examine representation, power, ideology, culture, and harm.

For the Puzzle Plan, this reading matters because digital misinformation shapes how marginalized students are seen. Students need tools to recognize stereotypes, missing context, biased language, fear-based narratives, and deficit framing.

A classroom shaped by this reading would ask:

- Who is represented?
- Who is missing?
- What assumptions are being made?
- What language creates fear, blame, pity, or distance?
- What historical or cultural context is missing?
- Who benefits if this story is believed?
- What counter-narrative could be created?

This reading connects digital literacy to culturally historically responsive literacy because it teaches students to read media systems critically, not just decode text.

## Short Notes on the Remaining Readings

### Knobel & Lankshear (2014)

This reading helps define the broader field of new literacies. It supports the idea that literacy changes as technologies, tools, platforms, and communities change. For the Puzzle Plan, it provides the foundation: if literacy is socially and technologically evolving, schools must stop treating print-only literacy as the only valid form of intelligence.

### Brooks & Frankel (2020)

This reading is useful for challenging deficit perspectives. It highlights adolescents' transnational digital literacy practices and helps teachers recognize that students' out-of-school literacies are evidence of knowledge, identity, and cultural intelligence. This is especially important for CLD and multilingual students.

### Serpagli & Mensah (2021)

This reading adds a STEM and gender lens by examining social media in an all-girls science classroom. It suggests that social media can support science learning when used intentionally. The caution is that teachers should not assume all students are automatically comfortable, safe, or empowered online. Digital natives still need scaffolding, boundaries, accessibility, and critical reflection.

## Explore: Digital Citizenship Lessons

Digital citizenship should not only mean online safety. It should also include ethical participation, privacy, access, credibility, kindness, boundaries, identity, and responsibility.

Through the Puzzle Plan lens, digital citizenship should ask:

- Who is protected online?
- Who is harmed online?
- Who is believed online?
- Who gets represented fairly online?
- Who gets access to digital participation?
- What supports do students need before they can participate safely and meaningfully?

This reframes digital citizenship as care, access, and justice.

---

## Watch: Dr. Nichole Pinkard on Digital Literacy

Dr. Nichole Pinkard's work is useful because it frames digital literacy as an ecosystem of learning opportunities rather than a single classroom skill. Students do not become digitally literate simply by using devices. They need opportunities to create, critique, collaborate, remix, design, publish, and receive feedback.

For the Puzzle Plan, this connects to the idea that students need pathways. Digital literacy develops through relationships, tools, mentors, communities, and authentic production opportunities. Teachers should recognize students' digital practices as meaningful literacy practices, not just distraction.

## Cross-Reading Synthesis

Across the readings and media, Week 5 makes four major arguments:

1. **Digital literacy is social, not only technical.** Students use digital tools to communicate, belong, interpret, create, resist, and participate.
2. **Digital literacy can reproduce or interrupt inequality.** Tools are not automatically equitable; design and pedagogy matter.
3. **Students already have digital literacies schools often miss.** Teachers should connect students' existing practices to academic, ethical, and critical learning.
4. **Critical media literacy is protective literacy.** Students need tools to identify stereotypes, misinformation, manipulation, exclusion, and deficit narratives.

---

## Future Teaching Moves

Based on the Week 5 materials, future teaching should include:

1. Multiple modes of response: writing, speaking, drawing, video, audio, coding, building, and visual design.
2. Peer teaching: students' digital strengths become classroom resources.
3. Critical media analysis: students ask who is represented, who is missing, who benefits, and what assumptions are normalized.
4. Counter-narrative production: students create media that corrects, complicates, or humanizes deficit stories.
5. Digital citizenship as care: privacy, boundaries, communication norms, and safety are taught directly.
6. Cultural and linguistic bridging: students use home languages, community knowledge, and transnational experiences in digital work.
7. Accessibility-first design: teachers plan for captions, templates, flexible pacing, visual supports, audio options, and role-based collaboration.
8. Technology as meaning-making: digital tools are used when they deepen access, agency, collaboration, or critical understanding.

## Placement Connection

This module connects directly to the teaching placement. In Kindergarten circuit work, students learned through touch, observation, shared vocabulary, and collective excitement. In Grade 4 Minecraft, students became peer teachers when they helped classmates complete the task. In Grade 5 airplane and fan-circuit work, students participated through roles, design, building, and problem-solving.

These examples show that digital and media literacy are not limited to screens. Students were reading systems, interpreting tools, collaborating, explaining, troubleshooting, and making meaning through action.

---

## Final Takeaway

Digital and media literacy are not optional extras. They are central to how students participate in the world. For the Puzzle Plan, digital literacy is a bridge between access and agency. It helps students who are often misread by schools show what they know, connect with others, critique harmful narratives, and create new forms of meaning.

> Do not ask only whether students can use technology. Ask whether digital literacy helps them become more visible, more critical, more connected, more protected, and more fully themselves.
